A Brief History of Deforestation in the Amazon

Satellite records of annual deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon, from 1988 to 2025. And why it increased from 2018 to 2022.

Square kilometres of forest cleared between 1988 and 2025, close to the size of Spain.

Brazil’s space agency, INPE, has measured Amazon deforestation by satellite since 1988 through a programme called PRODES. Its yearly figure covers August of one year through July of the next. The bars below show the area cleared each year, and the line shows the running total.
Approximately 15% of the Amazon has been cut.

The 2004 drop

Clearing reached 29,059 km² in 1995 and passed 25,000 km² again in 2003 and 2004. After 2004 it fell fast, to 4,571 km² in 2012, the lowest value in the whole series.
This is due to a combination of factors, including Brazil beginning to use the DETER program to target illegal deforestation (source) , the seizure of cattle found on illegally cleared land (source), the expansion of protected Indigenous areas (source), and grain traders refusing to buy soy grown on recently cleared land (source).

The rebound after 2018

The annual figure rose from 7,536 km² in 2018 to 9,762 km² in 2019, then climbed back above 10,000 km² in 2021 and 2022. The 2022 value of 11,600 km² was the highest since 2008. The rise had started earlier, though: clearing crept up from the 2012 low, after a 2012 law reform sent a signal of weaker commitment to protecting the forest (source).

Researchers mostly point to weaker enforcement after 2018. One study found that embargoes and asset confiscations dropped by 59% and 55% in 2019 and 2020 (source). A Nature paper found that fines paid fell by 74% and 89% in those same two years (source). A separate study of 2019 and 2020 found that only 1.3% of deforestation alerts matched an Ibama infraction notice or embargo (source).

Bolsanaro's government ran military operations in the Amazon and banned most fires during the dry season rather than use economic means of punishing deforestation. (source). Brazil's Defense Ministry said the deployment involved 2,090 people a day (source). Critics argued that the army would not address the systemic causes of deforestation, such as land grabbing, and that clearing kept rising during the operations.

The post 2022 drop

Deforestation fell to 7,952 km² in 2023, 6,288 km² in 2024 and 5,264 km² in 2025, about 16% below the year before. The 2025 figure is the lowest since 2014, though still above the 2012 low. This is largely due to the same reasons as before: the government relaunched the PPCDAm action plan in 2023 (source), Brazil's government issued more fines and embargoes against illegal clearing (source), the continued seizure of cattle found on cleared land (source), and new Indigineous reservations were created on rainforest land (source).

What the world loses

Amazon deforestation affects people and ecosystems far beyond Brazil; from local air quality to the risk of incomprehensible biodivervisty loss

Carbon and climate. The Amazon stores a large amount of carbon, and clearing and burning release it. Air measurements over the region found net emissions averaging nearly 300 million tons of carbon a year across the area sampled, roughly the emissions of France. (source). These figures include fire and forest degradation as well as clearing.

Rainfall. Up to half of the rain falling in the Amazon basin is generated within the basin itself, as trees recycle moisture back into the air. Losing forest in one area can therefore dry out areas far away, and can delay the wet season and threaten farming across South America (source). One study attributed nearly three-quarters of the decline in dry-season rainfall since 1985 to deforestation (source).

Wildlife. About one in ten of the world’s species live in the Amazon. Fires linked to clearing and drought have effected the habitat of over 77% of the region’s threatened species (source).

Human health. Forest fires raise air pollution, which one report says affected 24 million people in the region (source). Indigenous peoples, children and the elderly face the highest risks, along with food insecurity and the spread of infectious disease (source).
